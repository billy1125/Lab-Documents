# Lab Orientation

## 1. Let's Be Clear Up Front

The following are the basic bottom lines.

### 🙅‍♂️ Things I Will Not Ask You to Do

- I will not ask you to clean my personal office  
- I will not ask you to handle any personal matters unrelated to research (e.g., helping with housework, running personal errands)  
- I will not ask you to do unpaid work unrelated to research  
- I will not ask you to work unreasonable hours over long periods (e.g., staying up late for long stretches, no days off; of course, if you want to travel during winter or summer break, that's OK, but I'd like you to let me know first)
- I will not ask you to complete non-urgent research work on national holidays or long weekends; if there is a genuine emergency, I will explain the reason in advance
- I will not create pressure in indirect ways that make you feel "not working on holidays means you're not trying hard"
- I will not use social media to ask you to work outside working hours or on holidays (in return, please also respect each other's rest time)  
- I will not use emotion or pressure as my main way of managing (e.g., scolding or humiliation; though I do sometimes talk too much, so please bear with me)  
- I will not take credit for your research results or ignore your contributions
- I will not force you to take part in personal or external activities unrelated to research  
- I will not ask you, for my own personal needs, to provide technical support or labor unrelated to academic work 

> For details, see Section 4.7 "About Your Physical and Mental Well-being," Section 5.5 "Basic Lab Maintenance," Section 5.6 "If You Receive an Assistantship Stipend," and Section 8 "Attribution of Your Research Contributions."

### 🚫 Basic Bottom Lines (Please Make Sure You Understand)

#### Academic Bottom Line

If any of the following occurs, I will not wait to "discuss room for improvement"; I will directly consider ending the advising relationship:

- **Any form of research misconduct (fabrication, falsification, plagiarism)**  
- **Deliberately hiding data problems or forging research records**  
- **Violating academic integrity regulations**  

> This is the basic threshold for research work.

> For details, see Section 4.6 "Be Honest About Research Results," Section 5.7 "Principles for Using AI Tools," and the [Research Record Guide](Research-Record-EN.md).

#### Performance Bottom Line

I will take the following situations seriously, and will first talk with you to confirm whether there is willingness and room to improve:

- **No progress over a long period and no willingness to improve**  
- **Being out of contact for a long time or lacking a sense of responsibility**  
- **Staying passive and not moving your own research forward at all**  
- **Clearly affecting lab operations or other students**  

##### Additional Notes

- I understand that everyone runs into difficulties; **problems can be discussed**  
- But the condition is: you are willing to face the problem and try to improve  

> Everyone in this lab needs to take their own research and each other's time seriously.

> For details, see Section 4.1 "Take Responsibility," Section 4.2 "Make Your Work 'Visible'," Section 5.1 "Weekly Progress Reports," and Section 5.4 "A Deadline Is a Commitment."

#### Procedure for Ending the Advising Relationship

- Except for academic misconduct (fabrication, plagiarism, etc.), before I seriously consider ending the advising relationship, **I will give you at least one advance notice, both verbally and in writing**, explaining the specific problems and the deadline for improvement
- If we both agree that continuing to work together no longer makes sense, I will help you understand the administrative procedures that follow (changing advisors, keeping your student status, etc.), rather than simply cutting ties
- **Whatever the reason for ending the relationship, I will not announce it suddenly at a critical point (such as right before your thesis defense or at the end of a semester)**, so that you have enough time to handle what comes next in your studies

#### Conduct Bottom Line

This lab is committed to maintaining a professional, equal, and mutually respectful academic environment. Lab members (including peers, senior and junior students, and between faculty and students) must strictly follow these conduct bottom lines:

- No discrimination or harassment: Any form of discrimination, bullying, or harassment (including sexual harassment) is absolutely prohibited.
- No abuse of power: Using differences in status, the advising relationship, or academic authority to coerce or apply improper pressure is not allowed.
- Fair treatment: All members must be ensured that their academic participation and resource allocation are not affected by non-professional factors, so that treatment remains fair. 

> If you encounter any of the above in this lab or in any other academic setting, you can tell me directly, or seek help through the relevant channels the university provides.

> For details, see Section 4.5 "If You Feel Something Is Unreasonable."

### Graduation Requirements

- Time: I expect you to graduate in about 2 years; 3 years is also fine; in most cases I do not recommend going beyond 3 years  
- Thesis: High originality is not the main threshold, but you must go through the full path of a master's thesis research project yourself, write a complete thesis, and pass the thesis defense  
- Topic: A topic others have worked on is acceptable, but it cannot just be "copying a paper and doing it again"; you must add your own adjustments  
- You must lead and actually carry out the core research work  
- Every student must submit to an academic conference or take part in a competition  
- For students who choose Path C (Academic Track), the standard is higher: you must submit to an academic journal at least once before graduating (acceptance is not required)  

> For details, see Section 6 "Research and Graduation."

## 2. Who Might This Environment Suit?

This lab is still in its early stages, so things may be somewhat different from what you imagine life in a well-established lab to be:

- Equipment is still being gradually acquired (resources will improve step by step as the lab develops)    
- There are no senior students who can directly guide you  
- Many systems are still being set up
- Early resources (such as assistantship funding or student work pay) may be relatively limited  

If you have the following traits, this lab will likely suit you better:

- Not afraid of uncertainty; willing to figure things out as you go  
- Don't like having your path completely laid out for you (I will help you correct course)  
- Willing to learn on your own initiative and find information yourself  
- Interested in "building something from 0 to 1"  

This place is more like "a place with freedom to explore, find problems, and solve them," rather than walking a path that has already been paved.

On the other hand, if what you are hoping for is:

- The professor assigns a clear topic and method, and you carry it out in the set direction  
- Senior students to lead you, so you can draw on existing experience  
- Directly taking over stable, mature research built by senior students  
- A complete, lively team atmosphere from the start  

then other labs that are already well established may better match your expectations.

## 3. What Might Doing Research Here Feel Like?

You will not be handed an "already designed topic." Instead, you will do things like:

- Read papers, find problems  
- Think of methods  
- Try, revise, and try again  

You can think of it this way:

> No one hands you a path; you have to find the path yourself.

#### About Adjusting Research Direction

Adjusting direction is a normal part of the research process, but I will try my best to make sure that:

- Every adjustment has a clear reason, which I will explain verbally in our meetings
- If direction changes are frequent or you feel confused, please tell me proactively, and we will work together to confirm what the core problem is right now
- I will not tell you "this direction makes no contribution" only after you have put a lot of work into a direction I assigned, without giving you advance warning or a chance to discuss it

Of course, you may meet classmates or senior students working on similar topics. You can collaborate with each other, and if you find teammates like that, I very much encourage it.

But this depends on luck and cannot be forced, and others are not necessarily obligated to work with you. Please understand this from the start.

## 4. The Basic Lab Culture I Hope to Build

### 1. Take Responsibility
If a piece of research or work is yours, you are responsible for it all the way through.

- You can ask when you get stuck, but not without having tried at all  
- No one will keep reminding you what to do
- If you serve as a research assistant or TA, you must complete the basic work and keep track of it
- If something is about to go wrong or has already gone wrong, raise it as soon as possible so we can properly fix it; we can review what happened afterward. Please don't sit on it thinking no one will find out

### 2. Make Your Work "Visible"
It doesn't count just because you say you did it; it has to be verifiable or reviewable:

- Documented records
- Organized data analysis results
- Paper drafts
- Reproducible results  

### 3. Less Talk, More Experiments and Writing
Ideas matter, but what matters more is:

- Trying things quickly  
- Doing small-scale validation  
- Using results to adjust direction
- Putting it in writing  

### 4. Uncertainty Is Normal
At the start, research is bound to be somewhat chaotic, with unclear direction and repeated missteps. This is expected.

The point is not "everything goes smoothly from the start," but:

- Whether you keep moving forward  
- Whether you build up useful experience
- It doesn't need to be perfect from the start, but you should try to produce some results 

### 5. If You Feel Something Is Unreasonable

If you feel some arrangement is unreasonable:

- **Step one:** Discuss it with me directly and explain your thoughts and feelings; most issues can be cleared up through communication
- If it is **inconvenient or inappropriate to discuss it with me directly**, you can seek help through the following channels:
  - The university counseling center
  - The department chair or department office
  - The university's student grievance channels (see the university website)
- **You have the right to use these channels directly, without first getting my permission**

### 6. Be Honest About Research Results

Whether results are good or bad, they must be recorded and discussed truthfully:

- Results that don't match expectations are normal, not a problem
- But you must not repeatedly alter data or analysis methods because you "want a particular conclusion"  
- Failed experiments and results that don't come out need to be recorded and discussed just like successful ones  

If a result seems "off" or "can't be reproduced," tell me right away so we can find the cause together, rather than quietly ignoring or deleting it.

### 7. About Your Physical and Mental Well-being

Research pressure is real. Long periods of uncertainty, repeated failures, and changes in direction can all affect your mental health.

I want you to know:

- **You can tell me directly that you are under a lot of stress or can't hold on anymore.** This will not lower my opinion of you, and it is not a sign of weakness
- Feeling exhausted or losing motivation for research is normal. Saying so is more important than carrying it silently
- If you find it hard to talk to me, the university counseling center offers free counseling. I encourage you to use it; you don't need to wait until things are serious
- If you face serious life difficulties outside the lab, you can also tell me, and we can discuss adjusting the pace of your research together

> I believe that constantly staying up late and working under high pressure will, in the long run, hurt research quality rather than improve it. If you find yourself in this state, please stop and tell me.

### 7. Principles for Using AI Tools

This lab takes an open but cautious attitude toward generative AI (such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.). Applicants and members should follow these principles:

> Paid AI services (such as ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro, etc.) **are not a guaranteed resource**. Whether they are provided, and to which members, depends on the lab's funding situation that year. Please do not count them as an expected benefit of joining the lab.

- Assist, not replace: AI may only be used to help write code, polish wording, or compile information. It may not replace your own thinking, logical reasoning, or the writing of the core research.
- Responsibility for accuracy: Users are fully responsible for AI-generated content. Any incorrect information, fake references, or logical gaps produced by AI are treated as a reflection of your own research quality.
- Information security: Uploading the lab's unpublished source code, research data, or confidential materials to public AI platforms is prohibited, in order to protect research ethics and intellectual property.
- Transparent disclosure: When submitting an undergraduate project or thesis, if parts of it made substantial use of AI assistance, you should proactively state the scope and manner of that use.

## 6. Research and Graduation

In this lab, everyone's pace will be a bit different, and you can adjust according to your own plans (for example, combining it with an internship or exploring different directions).

Overall, though, I expect:

- Graduating in about 2 years; 3 years is also OK
- In most cases, I do not recommend going beyond 3 years  

If progress falls noticeably behind, we will discuss adjusting the direction or re-planning together.

### Common Development Paths

#### Path A: Standard Research (Steady Progress)

- First 6 months: find a research topic  
- Around 1 year: complete a draft thesis proposal  
- Around 1.5 years: build up results; submit to and get accepted at an academic conference, or take part in a competition
- Around 2 years: graduate  

Suits those who:

- Want to complete the degree steadily  
- Want a clear rhythm and schedule  
- Want to build basic research skills  

#### Path B: Research + Practice in Parallel (Flexible Development)

- First 6 months: read the literature and explore directions  
- Around 1 year: do an internship while confirming your research topic (preferably related to your internship work)  
- Around 1.5 years: complete a draft thesis proposal  
- Around 2 years: build up results; submit to and get accepted at an academic conference, or take part in a competition  
- Around 2–2.5 years: graduate  

Suits those who:

- Want to build industry experience at the same time  
- Are interested in implementation and application  
- Can manage their own time and progress  

#### Path C: Academic Track (Research Depth)

- First 6 months: read extensively and build foundations  
- Around 1 year: establish the research question  
- Around 1.5 years: complete a draft thesis proposal  
- Around 2 years: build up more complete results (with potential for journal submission)  
- Around 2.5–3 years: submit to an academic journal and graduate  

Suits those who:

- Are interested in research itself  
- Are considering going on to a PhD  
- Are willing to spend more time working on in-depth problems  

#### Path D: High Commitment, Fast Completion (Less Common)

- First 3–4 months: quickly settle on a topic  
- Around 6–9 months: complete the proposal and produce preliminary results  
- Around 1–1.2 years: complete the research; submit to and get accepted at an academic conference 
- Around 1.5 years: graduate  

Suits those who:

- Already have a foundation (undergraduate project / hands-on experience)  
- Can commit steadily over long periods  
- Are highly self-disciplined with clear goals  

Notes:

- This path is less common and is not recommended as the default plan  
- The topic needs to be relatively manageable and clearly scoped  

#### Additional Notes

This doesn't mean you can only take one particular path. If a path doesn't suit you, you can of course adjust; you can also take two at once, and you might even create a fifth path. What matters is:

- Whether you keep making progress  
- Whether you can gradually narrow down your research direction  
- Whether you can complete a full piece of work within the time available  

I will help you adjust direction, but only if you are moving forward, not standing still.

### Thesis Standards

> **Principle: High originality is not the main threshold.** My standard for a master's thesis is not "it must be highly innovative," but that you must go through the full path of a master's thesis research project yourself.

The topic doesn't need to be new; a topic others have worked on is fine.

> But if you work on a topic others have done, **it cannot just be "copying a paper and doing it again."** You must add the adjustments you think are needed.

For example:

- Moving it to a different context  
- Adjusting the research method  
- Adding research variables  

What I look at is whether you personally did each of the following stages:

- Finding and defining the research question  
- Reading and organizing the relevant literature  
- Designing the research method  
- Implementing, experimenting, or collecting data  
- Analyzing the results and explaining their meaning and limitations  
- Writing a complete thesis and passing the thesis defense  

Why I require this:

- The purpose of the master's stage is to train research ability, not to produce breakthrough results  
- Time is limited; chasing innovation easily leaves you stuck looking for a topic, squeezing the time left for implementation and writing  
- Only by doing every stage yourself will you know where the difficulties are, and be able to handle new problems independently later  
- Most students won't continue working on the same topic after graduating; what you take with you is this way of doing research  

Notes:

- You must lead and actually carry out the core research work. When you use others' data, code, methods, or help, you must clearly disclose and cite it  
- This standard corresponds to the "guaranteed-to-finish" topic described in Section 7; that topic also needs to be submitted to an academic conference or entered in a competition  
- The "potentially publishable" topic is an extra goal and does not affect graduation  

#### Additional Requirements for Path C (Academic Track)

If you choose Path C, the standard rises to "completing a study with potential for journal submission":

- Research question: It can't just be changing the context or adding/removing variables; there must be a clear theoretical, methodological, or empirical question  
- Literature review: In addition to summarizing prior research, you must point out research gaps and debates  
- Method design: It must be more rigorous, for example adding comparison models, robustness checks, or additional experiments  
- Discussion of results: You can't only answer "is it significant or not"; you must explain the meaning, the limitations, and possible reasons for inconsistencies with existing research  
- Completeness: Close to the point where it can be rewritten as a journal manuscript; it doesn't end when you pass the thesis defense  
- Submit to an academic journal at least once before graduating (acceptance is not required)  

Why the requirements for Path C are higher:

- I assume students who choose Path C genuinely want to go on to a PhD program  
- Many PhD programs require or encourage students to have a certain level of academic publications before they graduate  
- I hope that after you graduate, you can at least revise your master's thesis and submit it for publication  
- This training helps you become familiar with journal research and the submission process early. If you enter a PhD program later, it can reduce the time spent figuring out research and submission all over again.  

### Conference Submissions and Competitions

> Every student must submit to an academic conference or take part in a competition.

- In principle, you should complete an academic conference submission, with the goal of being accepted and presenting in person (in-person or online both count)  
- If you choose a competition, you need to complete a competition of a certain scale and submit complete work; winning an award is a higher goal  
- I encourage students to submit to international conferences  
- Discuss with me which conference to submit to before submitting  
- Your manuscript must be reviewed by me before it can be submitted  
- Submission timing depends on your development path; see "Common Development Paths" above  
- It's fine if you aren't accepted; keep submitting to another conference. But you may not submit the same manuscript to multiple venues at once; you can only resubmit elsewhere after it has definitely been rejected  
- If you still haven't been accepted after multiple submissions, discuss alternatives with me  
- Registration fees and travel expenses will be covered by lab funds, or I will help pay for them  
- Authorship and author order are determined by actual academic contribution; see Section 8 for the principles  

> Please don't assume you won't be accepted. Make the paper's content complete first; that is the part you can control.

## 8. Attribution of Your Research Contributions

While doing research in this lab, producing publishable results (such as academic conference papers, journal papers, competitions, or patents) is one of the important goals. Therefore, students are expected to follow the related plans and schedules during the research process, with completing concrete results as the aim.

When results are published, the author order principles are as follows:

- First author (student): actually leads the research and is responsible for the main content  
- Corresponding author (supervisor): provides research direction, method design, guidance on paper writing, and overall quality control of the content, and helps the student with the submission work
- Other authors: ordered by actual contribution

This arrangement is meant to make sure research contributions are accurately reflected in author order. It also means:

> Therefore, my hope that you will be first author is not only to recognize that you made the largest contribution, but also because I hope you will be seriously committed to the research content, rather than only taking part in some of the work.

#### On Recognizing Contributions

- "Carrying out the work in the direction I set" does not in itself constitute an independent contribution, but if the research results are built on your execution work, your contribution should be recorded accurately
- If I have concerns about your current level of contribution, **I will tell you directly in a meeting and give you a clear explanation of what to improve and a timeframe**, rather than waiting to raise it right before your thesis defense or on some other formal occasion
- Every student should clearly know their role in the overall research; if you have questions, ask directly

This includes moving the research forward, experiments and result validation, and writing and revising the paper. I will provide the necessary direction and technical guidance, but I will not do the core research work in place of the first author.

In addition, I want to make clear that:

- Publishing results is one of the important training goals of this lab
- The research process may require repeated revisions and additional experiments, and a fairly long time spent refining the work  
- In principle, every master's student must at least submit to and get accepted at an academic conference once, or take part in a competition
- If you plan to go on to a PhD, I recommend at least completing a journal submission (acceptance is not a hard requirement) 

The above are this lab's basic rules and expectations regarding research results and attribution of contributions. Please carefully consider whether you can meet these requirements before joining.

## 9. Research, Coursework, and Outside Part-Time Work

This lab respects students taking outside work (such as part-time jobs or tutoring) for personal financial needs. However, to ensure research quality and the value of your degree, please reach the following understanding about time allocation before joining.

### Basic Concept

In this lab, **if you are a regular full-time student, not a student in the in-service (working professionals) program**, your "**time commitment**" will directly affect your research quality and whether you can graduate smoothly.

Please first understand one thing:

> Research is not work that can be done in scattered bits of time

It usually requires:

- Long periods of continuous focus (several hours or more)  
- Ongoing thinking and repeated attempts  
- Steady progress that builds up over time  

### Research and Coursework Come First

Professional training during graduate school is an important investment in your future career. Therefore:

> **"Research and coursework should be your main priority"**

Specific recommendations:
* **Self-managed progress:** Make sure outside work does not affect or delay your research goals over the long term.
* **Allocation logic:** Avoid a time allocation pattern of "work first, research later."
* **No interference:** Demands from outside employers (such as being on call at any time) should not interfere with normal lab operations.

#### About Lab Resources and Financial Support

The lab is committed to creating a focused research environment and provides support within its means, but please understand the following correctly:

* **Funding uncertainty:** The lab's research funding (such as project grants and assistant allowances) is not a fixed salary; it **depends on the application and approval status of external research projects each year**.
* **Not guaranteed:** If projects are not approved or funding is cut in a given year, the lab may not be able to provide additional financial support. Please be sure to weigh your own financial resilience.
* **Internal priority:** When project funding allows, assistant positions and scholarships/stipends will be **allocated to students in this lab first**, to help students spend less time working off campus and focus on research.

#### Honest Self-Assessment and Recommendations

Before deciding to join this lab, please honestly assess whether the following match your current situation:

1. **Commitment:** I can make research my main commitment, not a secondary arrangement.
2. **Financial position:** I work part-time to maintain basic financial stability, not as a shift in the center of my life.
3. **Risk tolerance:** If the lab temporarily has no project funding to support me, I can still adapt and complete my degree.

> If your financial needs require a "long-term and stable" high income, or you currently need to prioritize work, I recommend choosing a lab with more time flexibility or a more mature management structure. That will better fit your current career plans.
